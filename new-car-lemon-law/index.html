New Car Lemon Law in Houston, TX

We review new-car repair records, warranty reports, and repeated vehicle defects.

Call (346) 214-1590 Ask a question

The warning light returns, the engine stalls, or the same noise comes back after another shop visit. A new vehicle can have a defect that appears only during acceleration, braking, startup, or a longer drive.

Repeated repairs are often the point where owners start keeping closer track of the vehicle. The repair order may describe a warning message, rough shifting, a dead battery, a steering pull, or a system that stopped working.

This is worth looking at now if the vehicle has already gone back for the same concern. Repair dates, mileage, warranty reports, and technician notes can become harder to sort out as more visits occur.

Warning lights and stalls

Owners often call about a check-engine light that returns after a repair, an engine that stalls at a stop, or a transmission that jerks between gears. Other complaints include a screen that goes black, a backup camera that fails, a battery that will not hold a charge, or brakes that make a grinding noise.

We also hear about a vehicle pulling to one side, a fuel gauge showing the wrong reading, water entering the cabin, or a key fob that will not recognize the vehicle. A dealer may use a different description on the repair order than the one an owner uses at the service counter.

The same driveway complaint can lead to different record questions. A warning light with a diagnostic code is not the same file as an intermittent stall with no code, even if both led to multiple repair visits.

Repair order file review

We begin by discussing the vehicle concern and the repair history. The first conversation usually settles basic points, such as whether the vehicle was purchased or leased, what problem brought it to the shop, and whether the same issue returned.

We then review the purchase or lease agreement, repair orders, dates the vehicle went into and out of the shop, and correspondence with the dealer. We compare the owner’s description to the technician’s findings, parts listed, mileage, warranty notation, and repair recommendation.

Some questions need the paperwork in front of us. A repair order may show a complaint was reported, a part was replaced, or a dealer could not duplicate the issue, and those entries can change how we evaluate the file.

Repair attempts and warranty

The number of repair attempts matters, especially where the repair orders describe the same warning light, mechanical fault, or system failure. Separate visits for unrelated items may read differently than repeated visits for one continuing defect.

Days out of service can also matter. We check the in-and-out dates on each repair order and whether the vehicle remained at the dealer for diagnosis, parts, testing, or a completed repair.

Written records are important because a verbal report at the service drive may not appear in the shop file. We also look at whether the issue was reported while warranty coverage was running, whether the vehicle was bought or leased, and who holds the full service history.

A strong review depends on the documents matching the vehicle’s actual history. The purchase papers, warranty materials, dealer correspondence, and repair orders each answer a different question.

Repair orders and VIN

Keep the repair orders from every dealer visit together. They are often printed at checkout or available through a dealer service portal, and they usually show the complaint, mileage, dates, technician notes, and work performed.

Have the purchase or lease agreement nearby as well. The VIN is commonly listed on that document, along with the vehicle description and transaction date.

Check the dates in and out of the shop against your calendar, text messages, or dealer emails. If the vehicle stayed at the dealer, note whether you received a loaner, rental, or pickup notice.
